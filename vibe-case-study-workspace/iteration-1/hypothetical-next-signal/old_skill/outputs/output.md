# Pre-draft decision package

**Recommended format:** Workflow / System

**Confidence:** Medium. An internal checklist is a packaged, repeatable capability, and use has extended beyond its creator. However, the current evidence does not yet explain the repeated onboarding friction, the judgment encoded in the checklist, the output people produced, or whether the three return uses happened independently. Those details are needed before this can become a defensible full case study.

**Alternative format:** Practice Note. Use this if the checklist mainly captures one useful onboarding lesson, or if the missing workflow, iteration, and ownership evidence cannot be supplied. A focused note could examine why a checklist that people can complete is not automatically a workflow a team owns.

**Story Thesis:** I turned **[specific repeated onboarding friction]** into an internal checklist that eight colleagues completed in facilitated walkthroughs. Three later returned to use it again, making the next design question concrete: what would need to change for the workflow to stop depending on its creator and begin to belong to the team?

This thesis is provisional. The bracketed trigger and the meaning of “returned” must be confirmed before publication.

**Author-reported evidence:**

- The artifact is an internal onboarding checklist.
- Eight colleagues completed facilitated walkthroughs.
- Three colleagues returned to use it again.
- Nobody has taught the workflow, invited new users, or redistributed it.

**Adoption level:** Facilitated trial, with a promising return-use signal. The eight walkthroughs demonstrate trial under support. The three returns are stronger than one-time completion, but they should not yet be labeled independent reuse unless the colleagues used the checklist on another onboarding occasion without the creator doing the work. There is no evidence yet of contribution or second-order adoption.

**Missing context:**

1. What specific onboarding failure or repeated friction triggered the checklist, and who experienced it?
2. What did onboarding require before the checklist existed, and where did people stall, improvise, or depend on another person?
3. What are the checklist's inputs, decision points, human judgments, and concrete outputs?
4. What did “facilitated” involve? Which actions did you perform that a colleague could not yet perform alone?
5. When the three colleagues returned, did they use it independently, for a new onboarding case, and to completion? What receipt can confirm this?
6. Did any walkthrough feedback change the checklist? If so, what was requested, what changed, and what became easier or clearer?
7. Who created the content, who validated it, who maintains it, and did AI or engineering materially shape any part of the workflow?
8. Which artifacts can be shown or anonymized: checklist states, completed outputs, messages about return use, version history, or facilitation notes?

**Recommended length:** Compact — 450–650 words.

**Length rationale:** The strongest available story is one clear arc: a packaged onboarding workflow moved from creator-led walkthroughs to limited return use, then reached a boundary where team ownership had not yet formed. That can be sharp and credible in a Compact case. Standard becomes worthwhile only if there are inspectable changes between versions, two or three consequential design decisions, and receipts showing how people used the checklist. Extended is not supported by the current evidence.

**Length options for user selection:**

- **Compact — 450–650 words:** Recommended for one focused arc: repeated friction → checklist → facilitated trial → return use → the next ownership threshold.
- **Standard — 800–1,100 words:** Choose this if you can provide a concrete before workflow, meaningful version evolution, two or three consequential decisions, and visual receipts for use and return behavior.
- **Extended — 1,300–1,800 words:** Worthwhile only if the checklist spans several phases or stakeholder constraints, has a rich iteration record, and later gains contribution, shared maintenance, teaching, or redistribution evidence.

No public draft should be written until a length is selected or the choice is explicitly delegated.

**Selected narrative modules:**

1. **Before Workflow** — make the recurring onboarding friction and creator dependency concrete.
2. **System Model** — show what enters the checklist, what it asks people to decide or do, and what successful completion produces.
3. **Workflow Walkthrough** — follow one real colleague through a facilitated run, including where support was necessary.
4. **Packaging and Return Use** — explain how colleagues accessed the checklist and what is known about the three subsequent uses.
5. **The Ownership Threshold** — distinguish successful use from shared ownership, then name the next behavior that would show the workflow beginning to travel without its creator.

The source-library patterns used here are **Repeated friction becomes a workflow**, **Packaging separates reuse from the creator**, **Contribution expands product scope**, and **Second-order use is stronger than reach**. They sharpen the sequence but do not supply project facts.

> **Adoption**  
> **3 colleagues returned to use the checklist again**

> **Constraint**

**Visual Evidence Plan:**

1. **Before workflow:** A factual diagram of the old onboarding steps, handoffs, and failure points. Label it as reconstructed if no contemporaneous artifact exists.
2. **System flow:** Trigger → checklist input → colleague action → decision or human judgment → completed onboarding output. Show where facilitation currently enters.
3. **Real artifact:** An anonymized checklist view paired with one completed output or end state.
4. **Trial receipt:** An anonymized attendance, completion, or session record supporting the eight facilitated walkthroughs.
5. **Return-use receipt:** An anonymized message, activity record, or version history supporting each of the three return uses and clarifying whether the creator was involved.
6. **Ownership threshold:** A clearly labeled analytical diagram showing the current evidence and possible next signals: facilitated trial → independent reuse → contribution/shared maintenance → teaching, invitation, or redistribution. This diagram explains the argument; it must not imply that the later stages have occurred.

**Where team ownership could begin:** The first credible shift is not another creator-led walkthrough. It is behavior that transfers agency: a colleague runs the checklist without facilitation, changes or improves it based on use, accepts responsibility for maintaining part of it, invites the next user, or teaches the workflow. One such event would be an early ownership signal; repeated contribution or second-order adoption would support the stronger claim that the workflow belongs to the team.

A useful next experiment would be to give one returning colleague a real onboarding occasion and permission to run, revise, and hand off the checklist without creator intervention. Capture where they still need help, what they change, and whether they bring in the next person. This tests the ownership boundary directly instead of treating wider distribution as proof.

**Claims to avoid or soften:**

- Do not call the eight facilitated walkthroughs self-serve adoption.
- Do not say “three repeat users” or “independent reuse” until the context and creator involvement are confirmed.
- Do not claim the checklist is team-owned, embedded, scalable, or organically adopted.
- Do not imply that anyone has contributed, taught, invited, championed, or redistributed it.
- Do not claim onboarding became faster, more effective, or more consistent without a defined comparison and outcome evidence.
- Do not describe availability as adoption or return use as second-order adoption.
- Do not present the possible ownership path as a result; frame it as the next hypothesis to test.

**Decision needed before drafting:** Choose Compact, Standard, or Extended. Based on the evidence currently available, Compact is recommended. Please also answer the minimum context questions above; if the workflow model and receipts remain unavailable, the safer direction is a Practice Note rather than a padded case study.
