# Pre-draft story direction

**Recommended archetype:** Workflow / System

**Confidence:** Medium. An internal checklist is a packaged, repeatable capability, and use has extended beyond its creator. However, the current evidence establishes facilitated trial and return behavior—not yet independent reuse, contribution, or second-order adoption. If the checklist does not encode a repeatable workflow or there is no concrete before-state and real output to show, the safer alternative is a short Practice Note.

**Story thesis:** I turned a recurring onboarding process into a checklist that eight colleagues could complete in facilitated walkthroughs; three returned to use it again. The next test is whether a returning user can bring someone new through the workflow without depending on me.

This gives the story a credible progression rather than an inflated adoption claim:

`Recurring onboarding friction → encoded checklist → facilitated walkthroughs → return behavior → next test: team-led use`

## Length options

- **Compact — 400–500 words:** Best for one clear arc: why the checklist was needed, what judgment it encoded, what the walkthroughs showed, and what would count as team ownership.
- **Standard — 500–600 words:** Worth choosing if you can show a meaningful version change, the feedback that caused it, and two or three concrete workflow decisions.
- **Expanded — 700–800 words:** Worthwhile only if the checklist has multiple phases, stakeholder or distribution constraints, several inspectable iterations, and enough receipts to support them.

**Recommended length:** Compact. The strongest current story is focused and honest: guided completion led to some return use, while team-led spread remains the next signal. A longer article would need more evidence and would risk padding this progression with generic process prose. Please choose Compact, Standard, or Expanded before drafting; if you delegate the choice, Compact is the default.

## Evidence map

**Observed evidence**

- None supplied or independently inspectable in the current request.

**Author-reported evidence**

- AR-01: Eight colleagues completed facilitated walkthroughs.
- AR-02: Three of those colleagues returned to use the checklist again.
- AR-03: Nobody has taught the workflow, invited new users, or redistributed it.
- AR-04: The artifact is an internal onboarding checklist.

**Inferred, not yet publishable as fact**

- The onboarding problem recurred across people or onboarding cycles.
- The checklist encodes judgment that previously depended on its creator or another facilitator.
- The three return sessions were self-initiated and completed without assistance.
- The walkthroughs or return sessions changed the checklist.

**Missing evidence**

- The concrete trigger and repeated before-workflow: who struggled, which steps repeated, and what the cost or risk was.
- The checklist's workflow model: trigger, inputs, decisions, actions, outputs, and points requiring human judgment.
- One real, anonymizable input/output example.
- What “returned to use it again” means: self-initiated or prompted, independent or facilitated, and for the same task or a new onboarding case.
- A walkthrough or usage receipt supporting the counts.
- Any feedback that visibly changed a later version.
- Ownership across the author, collaborators, AI, and checklist users.
- The packaging and access model: where the checklist lives, how someone discovers it, and what still requires the creator.

## Adoption diagnosis

**Current level:** Trial, with an encouraging return-use signal. The eight completions were facilitated, so they should not be described as self-serve adoption. The three returns are stronger than one-time completion, but they qualify as independent reuse only if those colleagues initiated and completed another real onboarding task without creator facilitation.

**The point where the workflow could begin to belong to the team:** when a returning user introduces, teaches, or redistributes it to a new colleague—or contributes a change that becomes part of the shared workflow—without the creator orchestrating the handoff. This has not happened yet and should appear only as a next signal or success criterion.

## Relevant source patterns

**MX-04 — Repeated friction becomes a workflow**  
Why it matches: The case will be strongest if it begins with the repeated onboarding sequence and shows how the checklist reconnects its fragmented steps.  
Project-specific candidate: “I reorganized the repeated onboarding steps into one checklist structured around what a colleague needs to complete and decide.”  
**Candidate status:** Hypothesis until the old workflow, checklist structure, and a real output are shown.

**MX-05 — Packaging separates reuse from the creator**  
Why it matches: The checklist is potentially a packaged form of onboarding judgment, but facilitated walkthroughs may still conceal creator dependency.  
Project-specific candidate: “The checklist made the onboarding sequence repeatable, but the walkthroughs showed that access to an artifact is not yet the same as independent use.”  
**Candidate status:** Hypothesis. Confirm what participants could complete without facilitation and which decisions still required help.

**MX-11 — Second-order use is stronger than raw reach**  
Why it matches: It directly sharpens the desired ownership threshold without upgrading current behavior into an outcome that has not occurred.  
Project-specific candidate: “Three colleagues returned to the checklist; the next meaningful signal is whether one of them brings a new teammate into the workflow without me.”  
**Candidate status:** The first clause is Author-reported; the second is a Hypothesis suitable only as a Next signal.

## Selected narrative modules

1. **The onboarding handoff that kept repeating** — establish the people, steps, and failure points before the checklist.
2. **What the checklist encoded** — show its sequence, decision rules, and where human judgment remains.
3. **Eight guided completions, then three returns** — report the two behaviors separately and clarify the degree of facilitation.
4. **From return use to team ownership** — end with the uncompleted threshold: invitation, teaching, contribution, or redistribution by a user.

## Visual receipts required

- **Before workflow:** A reconstructed and clearly labeled sequence of the old onboarding process, with actors, handoffs, and friction points.
- **Checklist model:** An inspectable overview showing trigger → required inputs → checklist decisions → human judgment → completed onboarding output.
- **Real input/output:** One anonymized onboarding example paired with its completed checklist or resulting artifact.
- **Facilitated-use receipt:** An anonymized attendance, session, or completion record supporting the eight walkthroughs.
- **Return-use receipt:** Timestamps, activity history, messages, or completed artifacts supporting the three returns and clarifying whether they were independent.
- **Future ownership receipt:** Do not fabricate this. If it occurs later, capture an anonymized invitation, teaching note, contribution trail, or redistribution message initiated by a user.

## Claims to avoid or qualify

- Do not call the eight walkthroughs self-serve adoption; they were facilitated.
- Do not call all three returns independent reuse until the author confirms they happened without facilitation and on a real subsequent task.
- Do not say the checklist “belongs to the team,” has “scaled,” or is “org-wide.”
- Do not imply that colleagues taught, invited, contributed, or redistributed it; the author explicitly says they did not.
- Do not claim time saved, fewer errors, better onboarding, or business impact without a consistent baseline and receipt.
- Do not call the checklist reusable solely because it is available; show what another person can complete without its creator.
- Do not infer that AI improved the workflow unless there is a concrete episode showing what AI did, what the author corrected, and how the result improved.

## Minimum context interview

1. What repeated onboarding problem triggered the checklist, who experienced it, and what happened in the old workflow?
2. What does the checklist encode beyond a list of tasks—decision rules, sequencing, examples, quality checks, or escalation guidance?
3. For the three returning colleagues, did they come back on their own, complete a new real onboarding task, and do so without your help?
4. Did anything observed or requested during the eight walkthroughs change the checklist? If so, what changed, and can you show the before/after plus the feedback receipt?
5. What did you personally define, what did collaborators or AI contribute, and what can be shown publicly in anonymized form?

Once the length and these high-value facts are confirmed, the public draft can make a precise claim: the checklist progressed from facilitated trial to return behavior, while team-led propagation remains the next test—not a completed outcome.
