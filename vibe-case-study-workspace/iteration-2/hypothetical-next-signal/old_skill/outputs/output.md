# Decision package: Internal onboarding checklist

## Recommendation

**Recommended archetype:** Workflow / System  
**Confidence:** Medium  
**Alternative:** Product Utility—or a Practice Note if the checklist does not encode a repeatable workflow or if the repeated onboarding problem cannot be evidenced.

**Why this archetype fits:** The promising story is not the checklist as a document. It is the attempt to turn onboarding knowledge into a repeatable capability that colleagues can use. Eight facilitated completions establish real trial, and three returns establish an encouraging repeat-use signal. They do not yet show that the workflow travels without its creator.

**Adoption level:** Trial, with author-reported return behavior. The eight walkthroughs were facilitated, so they are not self-serve adoption. The three returns are stronger than one-time trial, but they should not be classified as independent reuse until we know whether colleagues used the checklist on another occasion, for a real onboarding task, and without creator assistance.

## Story thesis

I turned internal onboarding knowledge into a checklist that eight colleagues completed in facilitated walkthroughs. Three later returned to it, suggesting value beyond the initial session; the next test is whether a returning user can bring someone else through the workflow without depending on me.

This thesis deliberately stops short of saying that the checklist already belongs to the team.

## Length options

**Compact — 400–500 words:** Recommended for the evidence currently available. It can carry one clean arc: recurring onboarding friction → encoded checklist → facilitated walkthroughs → return behavior → next ownership test.

**Standard — 500–600 words:** Choose this only if there is evidence for two or three consequential decisions—for example, a specific walkthrough observation that changed the checklist, a visible before/after version, and a concrete explanation of what the checklist encodes.

**Expanded — 700–800 words:** Worthwhile only if the project includes several phases, stakeholder or access constraints, multiple iteration receipts, and evidence about independent use or distribution. Current evidence would not sustain this length without padding.

**Recommended length:** Compact, pending the author's choice. No public article should be drafted until the author selects a mode or delegates the choice.

## Evidence map

### Observed

- None independently inspectable in the supplied project evidence.

### Author-reported

- The artifact is an internal onboarding checklist.
- Eight colleagues completed facilitated walkthroughs.
- Three colleagues returned to use the checklist again.
- Nobody has taught the workflow, invited new users, or redistributed it yet.

### Inferred

- The checklist may encode onboarding knowledge that previously depended on an individual or fragmented sources.
- Returning use may indicate that the checklist is useful beyond the walkthrough.
- The workflow could begin to belong to the team when another person carries it to a new participant without creator facilitation.

These interpretations require confirmation before they appear as facts.

### Unknown / missing evidence

- What repeated onboarding friction triggered the checklist, who experienced it, and what the previous workflow required.
- What the checklist actually encodes: steps, decision rules, examples, ownership, or escalation paths.
- Whether the three returning colleagues used it independently, for a real onboarding task, or for the same walkthrough scenario.
- Whether anyone completed the checklist end to end without the creator present.
- What changed after the walkthroughs, and which feedback or observed failure caused each change.
- What concrete output or onboarding outcome the checklist helped a colleague produce.
- The creator's role versus collaborators' contributions.
- Whether the checklist has a stable access point, owner, update path, and way for colleagues to contribute.
- What can be shown publicly or anonymized.

## Relevant source patterns and adaptations

### MX-11 — Second-order use is stronger than raw reach

**Why it matches:** The central open question is exactly where repeat use becomes team ownership. The current project has facilitated trial and return behavior, but no teaching, invitation, or redistribution.

**Candidate statement:** The next signal is not another creator-led walkthrough; it is a returning colleague bringing a new person through the checklist without me.

**Status:** Hypothesis. Use only as a Next signal or success criterion.

### MX-05 — Packaging separates reuse from the creator

**Why it matches:** A checklist can travel only if its logic, access, and instructions are usable without the person who assembled it.

**Candidate statement:** I began encoding onboarding knowledge into a checklist so colleagues could follow a consistent path rather than depend on an explanation from me.

**Status:** Unsupported as written. Confirm the old dependency and show what judgment the checklist encodes before publishing it.

### MX-10 — Distribution creates product trade-offs

**Why it matches:** Facilitation may be hiding setup, access, comprehension, or maintenance friction that will determine whether the checklist can spread.

**Candidate statement:** The walkthroughs tested whether colleagues could follow the checklist; the next phase must test whether they can find, run, and pass it on without creator support.

**Status:** Hypothesis. The first clause also needs a walkthrough protocol or receipt before publication as fact.

## Recommended narrative spine

1. **Why onboarding kept depending on a person** — Establish the repeated before-state with concrete actors, steps, and failure points.
2. **What judgment became a checklist** — Show the checklist's inputs, decisions, actions, and output; distinguish instructions from moments requiring human judgment.
3. **Eight guided runs** — Describe the facilitated walkthrough format and what it was designed to reveal. Do not frame completion as self-serve adoption.
4. **Three people came back** — Explain what “returned” meant, when it happened, whether help was required, and what task they were trying to complete.
5. **The handoff the project has not crossed yet** — End with the next ownership test: a returning user independently introduces, teaches, or redistributes the workflow to someone new.

This sequence keeps the public story causal: encoded workflow → guided trial → return behavior → explicit next threshold.

## Visual receipts required

- An anonymized view of the checklist showing its sequence and the judgment it encodes.
- A before-workflow diagram showing where onboarding previously depended on scattered information or individual explanation, if that is confirmed.
- A walkthrough receipt: anonymized session notes, completion record, or a small table defining participants, facilitation, task, and completion basis.
- A return-use receipt for the three colleagues, including date or interval, task, and whether creator help was involved.
- A version comparison only if walkthrough feedback visibly changed the checklist.
- Later, an invitation, teaching artifact, access trail, or contribution record if second-order adoption occurs. This receipt does not exist yet and must not be reconstructed as though it does.

## Minimum follow-up questions

1. What repeated onboarding problem did the checklist replace, and who previously carried the burden?
2. During the eight walkthroughs, what did you facilitate, and what counted as completion?
3. When the three colleagues returned, what did they use the checklist for, and did they need your help?
4. What changed in the checklist because of a walkthrough observation or request?
5. What would a colleague need—access, instructions, authority, or a contribution path—to bring a new person through it without you?

## Claims to avoid or qualify

- Avoid **“the team adopted the checklist.”** The evidence supports facilitated trial and return behavior, not team-wide adoption.
- Avoid **“three colleagues independently reused it.”** Independence and task context are not yet established.
- Avoid **“the workflow belongs to the team.”** No teaching, invitation, contribution, or redistribution has occurred.
- Avoid **“the checklist made onboarding faster, easier, or more consistent.”** No comparative outcome measure or user evidence has been supplied.
- Avoid **“the checklist removed dependency on me.”** Facilitated walkthroughs may demonstrate the opposite; this is the dependency still to test.
- Avoid converting the eight completions into a success rate unless the denominator, task, and completion criterion are known.

## Editorial boundary

The strongest current ending is an open but measurable transition, not a victory claim: return behavior shows that the checklist may have value beyond its first guided use; team ownership would begin when another colleague can carry the workflow forward—by introducing, teaching, adapting, or redistributing it—without the creator remaining the distribution layer.
